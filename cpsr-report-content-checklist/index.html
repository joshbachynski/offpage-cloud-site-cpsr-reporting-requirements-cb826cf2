CPSR report content checklist for CPSR reporting requirements

A CPSR report content checklist is a practical guide for confirming that a Cosmetic Product Safety Report includes every section needed for a complete safety assessment file. It helps a responsible person, assessor, or regulatory team verify that the report is organized, evidence-based, and ready to support market placement. The checklist should cover product identity, composition, toxicology, exposure, and the final safety conclusion.

CPSR report content checklist for the core report sections

The core CPSR report should contain the product identity, formula composition, ingredient functions, and a clear description of the finished cosmetic product. It should also show who prepared the assessment, what data were used, and which version of the formula the report applies to.
  • Product name and internal reference code
  • Responsible person or commissioning company
  • Assessor name, qualifications, and date of assessment
  • Full qualitative and quantitative formula
  • Product type, intended use, and target body areas
  • Batch or version identifier for the exact formula assessed

Start by checking that the report identifies the cosmetic product without ambiguity. The assessed formula must match the formula that will be manufactured, including ingredient percentages, raw material grades where relevant, and any colorants, preservatives, fragrances, or botanical extracts used in the final blend. A report that refers to a different version of the formula can create compliance gaps because the safety conclusion applies only to the assessed composition.

The report should also explain the product’s intended function and normal use conditions. A face cream, rinse-off cleanser, lip product, and aerosol spray may require different exposure assumptions, so the assessor must describe the product accurately. If the formula is for children, sensitive skin, leave-on use, or use near the eyes, that information should be obvious in the product description because it changes the risk evaluation.

CPSR report content checklist for ingredient and composition evidence

The ingredient section should allow a reviewer to trace every substance in the formula back to a known identity and role. It should include INCI names, concentrations, and supporting supplier or specification details where needed to confirm purity, restrictions, and classification status.

Ingredient documentation is one of the most important parts of a compliant report. Every raw material should be listed in a way that lets the assessor evaluate restrictions, impurities, and exposure. For complex blends, the checklist should confirm that sub-ingredients are broken down into their component parts rather than listed only by trade name. That is especially important for fragrance blends, preservative systems, and botanical preparations with multiple constituents.

A strong checklist also looks for consistency between the formula and the safety data used in the assessment. If the report relies on a supplier certificate, specification sheet, or impurity profile, those documents should align with the concentration and identity of the ingredient in the formulation. This is where many reports become weak: the formula may look complete on the page, but the supporting evidence for restricted ingredients, allergens, heavy metals, or contaminants may be missing or outdated.

CPSR report content checklist for restricted substances and impurities

The report should identify any restricted substances, impurities, or trace contaminants that affect safety conclusions. It should show whether the product stays within applicable limits and whether any ingredient requires additional justification, testing, or labeling consideration.

Use the checklist to confirm that the report addresses known restriction points, such as preservatives, colorants, UV filters, fragrances, and other ingredients with specific regulatory conditions. The assessor should explain how limits were verified and whether any ingredient presents a special concern due to sensitization, phototoxicity, inhalation risk, or cumulative exposure. If an ingredient is subject to purity criteria, the report should show the basis for accepting the supplied grade.

CPSR report content checklist for toxicological and exposure assessment

The toxicological section should connect ingredient hazard data with the product’s real-world exposure pattern. It should estimate how much of the product is used, how often it is used, where it is applied, and whether absorption or inhalation changes the risk profile.

This part of the report should not be a simple copy of hazard summaries. It needs to explain how the product is used by a consumer and what that means for safety. Exposure assumptions should match the product category, such as rinse-off versus leave-on, facial versus body application, or occasional versus daily use. A good checklist asks whether the report gives enough detail to justify the exposure model rather than relying on generic language.

The toxicological review should also identify any ingredient-specific concerns that require special attention. For example, the report should consider skin sensitization, eye irritation, systemic toxicity, repeated exposure, and relevant route-of-exposure issues. If the product is sprayed or likely to be inhaled, the assessment should address inhalation exposure directly. If the product is intended for damaged skin or mucosal areas, the report should explain why the formula remains acceptable under those conditions.

A useful checklist item is whether the report clearly states the margin of safety or equivalent reasoning used to reach the final conclusion, when that approach is applicable. The final opinion should be traceable to the data and assumptions in the report. If any uncertainty remains, the report should identify it and explain how it was handled, rather than leaving the reader to infer the conclusion.

CPSR report content checklist for packaging, labeling, and warnings

The report should include packaging and label information that can affect safety, such as container type, dispensing method, and warning statements. It should confirm whether the packaging supports the intended use and whether label instructions reduce foreseeable misuse.

Packaging can change how a cosmetic is used and how much exposure occurs. A pump, dropper, aerosol, tube, or jar each creates different handling patterns, so the report should describe the final packaging configuration. The checklist should confirm that the assessor reviewed the packaging material if it may influence stability, contamination risk, or product interaction.

Label content is also part of a complete safety file. Directions for use, warnings, storage instructions, and age restrictions can reduce avoidable risk. If the formula requires a warning because of fragrance allergens, eye-area caution, external-use limitations, or flammability concerns, the report should make that explicit. A report that ignores label-dependent risk controls is incomplete because the safety conclusion may depend on those instructions being present on the finished product.

CPSR report content checklist for supporting documents and final sign-off

The final report should be backed by supporting evidence that can be reviewed and updated when the formula or regulation changes. It should end with a clear safety conclusion, assessor identification, and enough supporting detail to defend the decision if requested by a regulator or auditor.

Good supporting documents usually include product specifications, raw material specifications, certificates of analysis where relevant, stability or compatibility data, microbiological information when needed, and any available test results that inform safety. The checklist should confirm that these documents are current and match the finished product. If the formula, supplier, or packaging changes, the report may need revision because the original safety opinion may no longer be valid.

The final sign-off should be easy to find and unambiguous. It should state whether the product is considered safe under the stated conditions of use and identify any restrictions or conditions attached to that conclusion. The assessor’s details, date, and version control help prove that the report is a controlled document, not a draft or incomplete summary.

When reviewing a report, the most useful question is simple: can another qualified reviewer follow the evidence from formula to exposure to conclusion without guessing? If the answer is yes, the report is likely well prepared. If the answer is no, the checklist usually reveals exactly where the gap sits, whether in ingredient identity, toxicology justification, packaging detail, or the final safety statement.

A reliable checklist also makes periodic review easier. Cosmetic formulas change, supplier documents expire, and regulatory expectations evolve, so a complete report should be treated as a living compliance document. That approach helps keep the safety assessment aligned with the product actually on the market.
